## 1. Number Sense Development (Ages 5-9)

### 1.1 What is Number Sense?

Number sense is the intuitive understanding of numbers, their magnitude, relationships, and how they are affected by operations. It differs fundamentally from rote memorization in several key ways:

| Number Sense | Memorization |
|--------------|--------------|
| Flexible thinking about quantities | Rigid recall of facts |
| Understanding of relationships | Isolated pieces of information |
| Multiple solution strategies | Single learned procedure |
| Transfer to new situations | Limited to practiced contexts |
| Connected knowledge network | Disconnected facts |

**Core Components of Number Sense (Berch, 2005; Jordan et al., 2006):**
- **Quantity discrimination:** Recognizing which of two sets has more
- **Counting principles:** Understanding cardinality, one-to-one correspondence, stable order
- **Number relationships:** Part-whole understanding, relative magnitude
- **Number patterns:** Recognition of sequences and regularities
- **Mental number line:** Spatial representation of numerical magnitude

### 1.2 Developmental Milestones by Grade

#### Kindergarten (Ages 5-6)

**Counting & Cardinality:**
- Counts to 100 by ones and tens
- Counts forward from any given number
- Understands that the last number counted represents the quantity (cardinality)
- Compares two groups using matching and counting

**Number Recognition:**
- Recognizes and writes numerals 0-20
- Subitizes (instantly recognizes) quantities up to 5
- Beginning to understand that numbers represent quantities

**Operations Foundation:**
- Understands addition as "putting together" and "adding to"
- Understands subtraction as "taking apart" and "taking from"
- Represents addition and subtraction with objects, fingers, drawings

#### Grade 1 (Ages 6-7)

**Place Value Introduction:**
- Understands that 10 ones equals 1 ten
- Composes and decomposes numbers 11-19 into ten ones and some more ones
- Understands two-digit numbers as bundles of tens and ones

**Addition & Subtraction:**
- Fluently adds and subtracts within 10
- Adds and subtracts within 20 using strategies
- Understands the relationship between addition and subtraction

**Comparison:**
- Compares two-digit numbers using >, <, =
- Uses place value understanding for comparison

#### Grade 2 (Ages 7-8)

**Extended Place Value:**
- Understands hundreds, tens, and ones
- Counts within 1000, skip-counts by 5s, 10s, and 100s
- Reads and writes numbers to 1000

**Fluency Development:**
- Fluently adds and subtracts within 100
- Adds and subtracts within 1000 using strategies based on place value

**Mental Math:**
- Mentally adds and subtracts 10 or 100
- Explains reasoning using place value and properties of operations

#### Grade 3 (Ages 8-9)

**Multiplication Foundation:**
- Understands multiplication as groups of objects
- Fluently multiplies and divides within 100
- Understands the relationship between multiplication and division

**Extended Operations:**
- Adds and subtracts within 1000 fluently
- Multiplies one-digit by multiples of 10

**Number Properties:**
- Uses properties of operations (commutative, associative, distributive)
- Identifies arithmetic patterns

### 1.3 Visual Magnitude Recognition Research

Research by Dehaene (1997, 2011) demonstrates that humans possess an innate "number sense" - an approximate number system (ANS) that allows intuitive understanding of quantities. Key findings:

**The Analog Magnitude System:**
- Numbers are mentally represented as magnitudes on an internal number line
- Discrimination follows Weber's Law: accuracy depends on the ratio between numbers
- Children can discriminate quantities before learning to count

**Visual Representations and Learning:**
- Spatial-numerical associations develop early (SNARC effect)
- Structured visual representations (e.g., ten-frames, base-10 blocks) support the transition from analog to symbolic understanding
- Visual magnitude representations predict later math achievement (Libertus et al., 2011)

**Implications for Design:**
- Use consistent spatial arrangements that reinforce magnitude
- Provide visual scaffolds that bridge concrete to abstract
- Design activities that strengthen the connection between symbol and quantity

### 1.4 Subitizing and Its Development

**Definition:** Subitizing is the ability to instantly recognize the quantity of a small set without counting (Kaufman et al., 1949).

**Two Types of Subitizing (Clements, 1999):**

1. **Perceptual Subitizing:** Instant recognition of small quantities (1-4), present from infancy
2. **Conceptual Subitizing:** Recognizing larger quantities by seeing them as composed of smaller groups (e.g., seeing 8 as two groups of 4)

**Developmental Progression:**
- Age 2-3: Subitizes quantities 1-2
- Age 4: Subitizes quantities up to 4
- Age 5-6: Begins conceptual subitizing with familiar patterns
- Age 7+: Conceptual subitizing with varied arrangements

**Research-Based Recommendations:**
- Use canonical patterns (dice, ten-frame) to support conceptual subitizing
- Progress from small quantities to larger composed quantities
- Vary spatial arrangements to build flexible recognition
- Flash activities strengthen rapid recognition skills

## 2. Montessori & Base-10 Visual Pedagogy

### 2.1 Base-10 Block Methodology

Base-10 blocks (also called Dienes blocks, after mathematician Zoltan Dienes) provide a proportional model where the size of each piece reflects its value:

| Material | Value | Visual Relationship |
|----------|-------|---------------------|
| Unit cube | 1 | Single small cube |
| Long/Rod | 10 | 10 unit cubes in a row |
| Flat | 100 | 10 rods arranged in a square |
| Cube | 1000 | 10 flats stacked |

**Research Support (Fuson & Briars, 1990; Hiebert & Wearne, 1992):**
- Proportional materials help children understand the multiplicative relationship between place values
- Physical manipulation supports mental imagery
- Trading activities (10 ones for 1 ten) build understanding of regrouping

**Digital Implementation Principles:**
- Maintain proportional relationships in visual design
- Allow grouping/ungrouping animations that show equivalence
- Provide both structured (grid) and unstructured (scattered) modes
- Include auditory and haptic feedback to reinforce actions

### 2.2 Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) Approach

The CRA sequence, based on Bruner's (1966) learning theory, provides a systematic progression:

**Stage 1: Concrete**
- Physical manipulation of real objects
- Multisensory engagement
- Building intuitive understanding through action

**Stage 2: Representational (Pictorial)**
- Drawings and visual models of concrete materials
- Mental imagery development
- Bridge between physical and symbolic

**Stage 3: Abstract**
- Symbolic notation (numerals, equations)
- Mental manipulation of quantities
- Flexible application to new problems

**Research Evidence (Witzel et al., 2003; Gersten et al., 2009):**
- CRA approach significantly improves math achievement
- Particularly effective for students with learning difficulties
- Each stage prepares students for the next level of abstraction

**Digital Application:**
- Start with virtual manipulatives that behave like physical objects
- Transition to static visual representations
- Connect visual models to symbolic notation
- Allow movement between levels based on student need

### 2.3 Montessori Golden Bead Materials

The Montessori golden bead material provides a concrete representation of the decimal system:

**Original Materials:**
- Individual golden beads (units)
- Bars of 10 beads (tens)
- Squares of 100 beads (hundreds)
- Cubes of 1000 beads (thousands)

**Pedagogical Principles:**
- Children physically experience quantity before learning symbols
- "The Bank Game" teaches exchange/regrouping
- Materials are self-correcting (children can verify through counting)
- Isolation of difficulty (one new concept at a time)

**Digital Equivalents:**
- Interactive beads that can be grouped and ungrouped
- Visual animation showing the "exchange" process
- Quantity verification through counting animations
- Color coding consistent with Montessori tradition (green for units, blue for tens, red for hundreds)

### 2.4 Research on Visual Representations of Quantity

**Effective Visual Models (Van de Walle et al., 2018):**

1. **Ten-Frames:** 2x5 arrays that build relationships to 5 and 10
2. **Rekenrek (Arithmetic Rack):** Rows of 10 beads (5 of each color)
3. **Number Lines:** Linear representation of magnitude
4. **Area Models:** Two-dimensional representations supporting multiplication
5. **Part-Part-Whole Diagrams:** Show composition relationships

**Research Findings:**
- Multiple representations deepen understanding (Ainsworth, 2006)
- Consistent visual models reduce cognitive load
- Interactive models outperform static images (Moyer-Packenham & Westenskow, 2013)
- Physical-to-virtual transfer is enhanced by design similarity

## 3. Composition & Decomposition

### 3.1 Number Bonds Research

Number bonds (also called number pairs or fact families) represent the part-part-whole relationship visually:

```
    8
   / \
  3   5
```

**Research Foundation (Baroody, 2006; Fuson, 1992):**
- Part-whole understanding is foundational for flexible arithmetic
- Number bond knowledge predicts computational fluency
- Children who understand number bonds develop more efficient strategies

**Developmental Progression:**
1. Bonds of numbers to 5
2. Bonds of numbers to 10 (critical benchmark)
3. Bonds of teen numbers (e.g., 15 = 10 + 5)
4. Bonds of larger numbers using place value

**Instructional Implications:**
- Emphasize "friends of 10" (pairs that sum to 10)
- Connect addition and subtraction through the same bond
- Use multiple representations (objects, pictures, symbols)
- Practice both composition (parts to whole) and decomposition (whole to parts)

### 3.2 Part-Part-Whole Understanding

Part-part-whole reasoning is the understanding that a quantity can be broken into parts and that those parts together make the whole.

**Key Concepts:**
- **Composition:** Combining parts to make a whole (3 + 5 = 8)
- **Decomposition:** Breaking a whole into parts (8 = 3 + 5)
- **Conservation:** The whole remains the same regardless of how parts are arranged

**Cognitive Benefits (Verschaffel et al., 2007):**
- Supports mental computation strategies
- Enables use of known facts to derive unknown facts
- Builds foundation for algebraic thinking
- Develops number flexibility

### 3.3 Multiple Representations of the Same Number

Understanding that numbers can be represented in many equivalent ways is crucial for mathematical flexibility:

**Examples for the number 8:**
- 8 ones
- 4 + 4
- 5 + 3
- 10 - 2
- 2 + 2 + 2 + 2
- Eight objects in various arrangements

**Research Support (Behr et al., 1983):**
- Multiple representations develop deeper conceptual understanding
- Translation between representations indicates robust knowledge
- Flexibility with representations predicts problem-solving success

**Digital Implementation:**
- Show multiple ways to build the same number
- Celebrate different valid solutions
- Provide tools for students to explore representations
- Connect representations visually

### 3.4 Research on Flexible Thinking with Numbers

**Characteristics of Flexible Thinkers (Threlfall, 2002; Star & Rittle-Johnson, 2008):**
- Use multiple strategies appropriately
- Choose efficient strategies based on number characteristics
- Understand why strategies work
- Transfer strategies to new situations

**Developing Flexibility:**
1. Exposure to multiple strategies
2. Comparison of different approaches
3. Discussion of when strategies are useful
4. Practice selecting appropriate strategies

**Game Design Implications:**
- Present problems that can be solved multiple ways
- Reward efficient strategies without penalizing valid alternatives
- Provide reflection prompts about strategy choice
- Vary number combinations to encourage strategy adaptation

## 4. Place Value Development

### 4.1 When and How Place Value Understanding Develops

Place value understanding develops gradually through several stages (Fuson et al., 1997):

**Stage 1: Unitary (Pre-Place Value)**
- Sees 52 as simply "fifty-two" without understanding the 5 represents 50
- Counts by ones only

**Stage 2: Initial Place Value**
- Understands that digits have different values based on position
- Can identify tens and ones but may not connect to quantity

**Stage 3: Intermediate Place Value**
- Understands tens as groups of ten ones
- Can compose and decompose numbers by place value
- Beginning mental computation

**Stage 4: Extended Place Value**
- Flexible understanding of place value relationships
- Can think of numbers in multiple ways (52 = 5 tens + 2 ones = 4 tens + 12 ones)
- Efficient mental strategies based on place value

**Timeline:**
- Kindergarten: Foundation through counting and grouping
- Grade 1: Initial place value with two-digit numbers
- Grade 2: Extended to three-digit numbers
- Grade 3: Consolidation and flexible application

### 4.2 Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1: Face Value**
- Error: Treating each digit by its face value (52 = 5 + 2 = 7)
- Cause: Insufficient concrete experience with grouping

**Misconception 2: Concatenation**
- Error: Writing "50" and "2" as "502" for fifty-two
- Cause: Incomplete understanding of positional notation

**Misconception 3: Reversal**
- Error: Writing 52 as 25 for "fifty-two"
- Cause: Confusion about digit order significance

**Misconception 4: Trading Errors**
- Error: Regrouping without understanding equivalence
- Cause: Procedural learning without conceptual foundation

**Misconception 5: Zero as Placeholder**
- Error: Treating zero as "nothing" rather than a positional placeholder
- Cause: Abstract nature of zero in place value system

**Prevention Strategies:**
- Extensive work with concrete grouped materials
- Explicit connections between groups and symbols
- Emphasis on trading as equivalent exchange
- Multiple representations of the same number

### 4.3 Visual Scaffolding Techniques

**Effective Scaffolds:**

1. **Place Value Charts:**
   | Hundreds | Tens | Ones |
   |----------|------|------|
   | 2 | 5 | 3 |
   - Clearly shows position of each digit
   - Can include visual models within cells

2. **Expanded Form Representations:**
   - 253 = 200 + 50 + 3
   - Shows value of each digit explicitly

3. **Proportional Materials:**
   - Base-10 blocks maintain size relationships
   - Support understanding of 10x relationship between places

4. **Non-Proportional Materials (with caution):**
   - Place value chips (same size, different colors)
   - Useful after proportional understanding established

5. **Arrow Cards:**
   - Overlay cards that show expanded form physically
   - 200 + 50 + 3 stack to show 253

### 4.4 Progression from Ones to Tens to Hundreds

**Recommended Progression (Clements & Sarama, 2014):**

**Phase 1: Ones Understanding (K-early Grade 1)**
- Counting collections up to 20
- One-to-one correspondence
- Cardinality principle

**Phase 2: Introduction to Tens (Grade 1)**
- Grouping activities (bundling straws, cupping objects)
- Recognizing groups of 10
- Teen numbers as "ten and some more"
- Two-digit numbers with base-10 materials

**Phase 3: Tens and Ones Fluency (Late Grade 1-Grade 2)**
- Trading/exchanging activities
- Mental addition/subtraction of 10
- Comparing two-digit numbers using place value

**Phase 4: Hundreds Introduction (Grade 2)**
- Extending grouping to hundreds
- Three-digit number representation
- Skip counting by 100s

**Phase 5: Three-Digit Fluency (Grades 2-3)**
- Operations with regrouping
- Flexible thinking about three-digit numbers
- Mental strategies with place value

## 5. Number Line & Ordering

### 5.1 Mental Number Line Formation

Research by Dehaene and colleagues demonstrates that humans develop a mental number line - a spatial representation of numerical magnitude (Dehaene et al., 1993).

**Key Findings:**

**Spatial-Numerical Association of Response Codes (SNARC Effect):**
- People respond faster to small numbers with left hand, large numbers with right hand
- Suggests automatic spatial coding of number magnitude
- Develops through education and cultural experience

**Mental Number Line Characteristics:**
- Initially logarithmic (compressed at larger values)
- Becomes more linear through education
- Cultural influences on direction (left-to-right in Western cultures)

**Educational Implications (Siegler & Booth, 2004):**
- Linear number line estimation predicts math achievement
- Instruction on number line placement improves understanding
- Board games with numbered spaces support development

### 5.2 Magnitude Comparison Strategies

**Developmental Progression of Comparison:**

**Stage 1: Perceptual Comparison**
- Compare quantities by visual inspection
- Limited to small, visible quantities

**Stage 2: Counting Comparison**
- Count both sets and compare counts
- Requires stable counting sequence

**Stage 3: Number Word Comparison**
- Know that "seven" is more than "five"
- Based on counting sequence knowledge

**Stage 4: Symbolic Comparison**
- Compare written numerals
- Use place value for multi-digit comparison

**Strategies for Multi-Digit Comparison (Fuson, 1988):**
1. **Front-end strategy:** Compare leftmost digits first
2. **Place value strategy:** Compare by place value position
3. **Number line reference:** Use known benchmarks (50, 100)
4. **Subtraction strategy:** Find the difference (less efficient)

### 5.3 Research on Ordering Activities

**Types of Ordering Tasks:**
1. **Sequencing:** Putting numbers in order (ascending/descending)
2. **Inserting:** Finding where a number belongs in a sequence
3. **Missing Number:** Identifying what number is missing

**Research Findings (Lyons et al., 2014):**
- Ordinality understanding (ordering) predicts math achievement
- Distinct from cardinality but equally important
- Ordering tasks activate different neural processes than comparison

**Effective Ordering Instruction:**
- Use physical number lines children can manipulate
- Include benchmark numbers (5, 10, 50, 100) as reference points
- Practice with varied number ranges
- Connect to real-world contexts (age, height, calendar)

### 5.4 Spatial-Numerical Associations

**Research on Space-Number Connections (Cipora et al., 2015):**
- Numerical and spatial processing share cognitive resources
- Early spatial skills predict later math achievement
- Number line training improves both spatial and numerical understanding

**Design Implications:**
- Use consistent left-to-right orientation for number lines
- Provide spatial landmarks/benchmarks
- Include visual estimation activities
- Connect spatial arrangement to numerical magnitude

## 6. Educational Game Design for Math

### 6.1 Spaced Repetition in Learning Apps

**Principles of Spaced Practice (Cepeda et al., 2006):**
- Distributed practice outperforms massed practice
- Optimal spacing depends on retention interval
- Increasing intervals strengthen long-term retention

**Implementation in Educational Games:**
- Track mastery of individual skills/facts
- Automatically schedule review based on performance
- Increase intervals for well-known content
- Decrease intervals after errors

**Recommended Algorithm Characteristics:**
- Initial presentation with immediate practice
- First review after 1-2 days
- Subsequent reviews with expanding intervals
- Adaptive adjustment based on accuracy and response time

### 6.2 Productive Struggle vs. Frustration

**Productive Struggle (Kapur, 2014; Warshauer, 2015):**
- Optimal challenge slightly exceeds current ability
- Struggle followed by success builds persistence
- Process-focused effort leads to deeper learning

**Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978):**
- Tasks should be achievable with appropriate support
- Scaffolding should fade as competence increases
- Peer and tool support can extend capability

**Indicators of Productive Struggle:**
- Engaged persistence despite difficulty
- Trying multiple strategies
- Seeking help appropriately
- Showing satisfaction upon success

**Indicators of Frustration:**
- Repeated unsuccessful attempts with same strategy
- Emotional distress (in digital: rage clicking, quitting)
- Avoidance behaviors
- Declining engagement

**Design Strategies:**
- Adaptive difficulty that responds to performance
- Just-in-time hints that scaffold without solving
- Multiple paths to success
- Celebrate effort and strategy, not just answers

### 6.3 Feedback Loops That Promote Learning

**Effective Feedback Characteristics (Hattie & Timperley, 2007):**
- **Immediate for verification:** Confirm correct/incorrect quickly
- **Delayed for elaboration:** Allow processing time for explanations
- **Task-focused:** Address the work, not the person
- **Forward-looking:** Guide next steps

**Feedback Levels:**
1. **Task Level:** Correct/incorrect, how to fix
2. **Process Level:** Strategies used, alternative approaches
3. **Self-Regulation Level:** Self-monitoring, next steps
4. **Self Level:** Praise about the learner (least effective for learning)

**Digital Implementation:**
- Visual feedback for immediate verification
- Animated explanations for errors
- Progress indicators for motivation
- Strategy suggestions after multiple attempts

**Feedback Timing:**
- Immediate feedback for fact practice
- Delayed feedback for problem-solving (allow productive struggle)
- Summary feedback at session end
- Progress feedback over time

### 6.4 Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation

**Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000):**
Three basic psychological needs for intrinsic motivation:
1. **Autonomy:** Sense of choice and control
2. **Competence:** Feeling effective and capable
3. **Relatedness:** Connection to others

**Intrinsic Motivation in Math Games:**
- Challenge that matches skill level
- Curiosity and exploration opportunities
- Meaningful choices
- Sense of mastery and growth

**Extrinsic Motivation Considerations:**
- Points and badges can undermine intrinsic motivation if overused
- External rewards effective for less interesting tasks
- Informational rewards (showing progress) better than controlling rewards

**Recommended Approach:**
- Primary focus on intrinsic motivators (challenge, curiosity, competence)
- Use rewards that provide information about progress
- Avoid rewards that feel controlling
- Celebrate learning and effort over performance
- Provide meaningful choices where possible

## 7. Application to NumberSense Games

### 7.1 "Build the Number" - Composition/Decomposition with Blocks

#### Learning Objectives
Based on the research above, "Build the Number" should target:

1. **Primary Objectives:**
   - Understand place value through concrete-representational connection
   - Develop composition and decomposition fluency
   - Build mental models of base-10 structure

2. **Secondary Objectives:**
   - Strengthen number bond knowledge
   - Develop flexible thinking about numbers
   - Connect visual representations to symbolic notation

#### Research-Based Design Recommendations

**Visual Design:**
| Recommendation | Research Basis |
|----------------|----------------|
| Use proportional base-10 blocks where tens are visibly 10x ones | Fuson & Briars (1990): Proportional models support understanding |
| Include animation for grouping 10 ones into 1 ten | Montessori exchange principle; Hiebert & Wearne (1992) |
| Show multiple correct configurations for the same number | Multiple representation research (Behr et al., 1983) |
| Use consistent color coding (ones/tens/hundreds) | Reduce cognitive load, Montessori principles |
| Provide ten-frame overlays for ones | Supports subitizing and relationship to 10 |

**Interaction Design:**
| Recommendation | Research Basis |
|----------------|----------------|
| Allow both building up and breaking apart | Part-part-whole bidirectional understanding |
| Enable drag-and-drop manipulation | CRA concrete stage principles |
| Provide snap-to-grid functionality | Structured representations support learning |
| Include "shake to scatter/organize" feature | Multiple arrangement exposure (Clements, 1999) |
| Allow automatic regrouping with animation | Visual support for exchange concept |

**Feedback System:**
| Recommendation | Research Basis |
|----------------|----------------|
| Immediate visual confirmation of correct builds | Verification feedback (Hattie & Timperley) |
| Show running total as blocks are placed | Real-time connection to quantity |
| Celebrate multiple valid solutions equally | Flexible thinking development |
| Provide hints after 2-3 unsuccessful attempts | Productive struggle threshold |
| Use animated demonstrations for teaching moments | Worked examples research |

**Difficulty Progression:**
```
Level 1: Build numbers 1-10 with ones only
Level 2: Build numbers 11-20 with ones, introduce tens
Level 3: Build two-digit numbers with provided tens and ones
Level 4: Build numbers with choice of blocks (composition)
Level 5: Build same number multiple ways (flexibility)
Level 6: Extend to three-digit numbers
Level 7: Decomposition challenges (given number, break apart)
Level 8: Exchange challenges (trade 10 ones for 1 ten)
```

**Adaptive Features:**
- Track speed and accuracy by number range
- Identify place value misconceptions through error patterns
- Adjust available blocks based on current learning level
- Spiral review of mastered content with expanding intervals

#### Sample Learning Sequences

**Sequence A: Introduction to Tens**
1. Build 8 with ones (foundation)
2. Build 10 with ones (benchmark)
3. Watch animation: 10 ones become 1 ten
4. Build 12 as 1 ten and 2 ones
5. Practice teen numbers
6. Build 12 in two ways (12 ones vs. 1 ten + 2 ones)

**Sequence B: Flexible Composition**
1. Build 35 with provided blocks
2. Build 35 with choice of blocks
3. Build 35 at least 2 different ways
4. Compare: Which way used fewer blocks?
5. Apply to new numbers

### 7.2 "Sort the Numbers" - Ordering and Magnitude Comparison

#### Learning Objectives

1. **Primary Objectives:**
   - Develop mental number line representation
   - Build magnitude comparison strategies
   - Understand ordinality (ordering relationships)

2. **Secondary Objectives:**
   - Strengthen number recognition fluency
   - Develop benchmark number understanding
   - Connect spatial position to numerical magnitude

#### Research-Based Design Recommendations

**Visual Design:**
| Recommendation | Research Basis |
|----------------|----------------|
| Use horizontal number line (left-to-right orientation) | SNARC effect, cultural convention |
| Include visual magnitude indicators (size, length) | Support approximate number sense |
| Show benchmark numbers (0, 5, 10, 50, 100) as fixed references | Anchor points support estimation |
| Use consistent spacing proportional to value differences | Linear number line development (Siegler & Booth) |
| Provide graduated backgrounds showing magnitude zones | Visual scaffolding for magnitude |

**Interaction Design:**
| Recommendation | Research Basis |
|----------------|----------------|
| Drag numbers to positions on number line | Active manipulation supports learning |
| Allow repositioning and adjustment | Self-correction opportunity |
| Include snap points for exact positioning | Scaffolded accuracy |
| Provide "check position" verification | Immediate feedback for verification |
| Enable comparison mode (two numbers at a time) | Reduce cognitive load |

**Game Modes:**
1. **Number Line Placement:** Place single numbers on an empty or partially-filled line
2. **Ordering Challenge:** Arrange 3-5 numbers from least to greatest
3. **Missing Number:** Identify which number belongs between two others
4. **Comparison Battles:** Choose the greater/lesser of two numbers quickly
5. **Estimation Station:** Estimate position without exact placement

**Feedback System:**
| Recommendation | Research Basis |
|----------------|----------------|
| Visual feedback showing correct position | Spatial-numerical connection |
| "Getting warmer/colder" for near misses | Productive struggle support |
| Show magnitude comparison visually (bars, circles) | Multiple representation |
| Highlight place value when useful for comparison | Strategy instruction |
| Provide strategy tips based on error patterns | Adaptive scaffolding |

**Difficulty Progression:**
```
Level 1: Order numbers 1-5
Level 2: Order numbers 1-10
Level 3: Place numbers on 0-10 number line
Level 4: Order numbers 1-20
Level 5: Place numbers on 0-20 number line
Level 6: Compare two-digit numbers (same decade)
Level 7: Compare two-digit numbers (different decades)
Level 8: Order three two-digit numbers
Level 9: Number line 0-100 with benchmarks
Level 10: Extend to three-digit numbers
Level 11: Missing number challenges
Level 12: Estimation challenges (no exact placement)
```

**Adaptive Features:**
- Track accuracy by number range
- Identify comparison strategy use (counting vs. place value)
- Adjust number ranges based on mastery
- Include targeted review of challenging comparisons
- Build personalized fact fluency for common comparisons

#### Sample Learning Sequences

**Sequence A: Building the Mental Number Line**
1. Arrange physical blocks 1-5 in order
2. Match numerals to positions 1-5
3. Place numerals on number line 1-5
4. Extend to 1-10 with benchmark at 5
5. Place numbers relative to 5 ("more than 5" or "less than 5")
6. Precise placement 0-10

**Sequence B: Two-Digit Comparison**
1. Review: Compare one-digit numbers
2. Introduce tens comparison (20 vs. 40)
3. Compare same-tens numbers (23 vs. 27)
4. Compare different-tens numbers (23 vs. 47)
5. Compare "tricky" pairs (29 vs. 31)
6. Strategy discussion: When is place value helpful?

### 7.3 Integration Between Games

**Connecting Learning Across Games:**

Both games reinforce complementary aspects of number sense:

| Concept | Build the Number | Sort the Numbers |
|---------|------------------|------------------|
| Place Value | Constructing numbers shows place value structure | Comparing numbers uses place value reasoning |
| Magnitude | Building shows quantity | Ordering develops magnitude sense |
| Number Relationships | Multiple representations | Relative position on number line |
| Benchmarks | Building to 10, 100 | Reference points on number line |

**Cross-Game Features:**
- Consistent visual design language
- Shared progress tracking
- Numbers practiced in one game appear in the other
- Achievement system spans both games
- Parent/teacher dashboard shows combined progress

**Suggested Session Structure:**
1. Begin with 5 minutes of "Build the Number" (concrete foundation)
2. Transition to "Sort the Numbers" (apply understanding)
3. Alternate focus based on learning goals
4. Review sessions mix both activities

## Appendix A: Quick Reference Design Checklist

### "Build the Number" Checklist

- [ ] Proportional base-10 blocks (tens visibly 10x ones)
- [ ] Smooth grouping/ungrouping animations
- [ ] Multiple valid solutions accepted and celebrated
- [ ] Consistent color coding for place values
- [ ] Ten-frame support for ones
- [ ] Running total display
- [ ] Adaptive difficulty progression
- [ ] Hint system after productive struggle period
- [ ] Spaced repetition for review
- [ ] Error pattern tracking for targeted intervention

### "Sort the Numbers" Checklist

- [ ] Horizontal, left-to-right number line
- [ ] Clear benchmark indicators (5, 10, 50, 100)
- [ ] Proportional spacing reflecting magnitude
- [ ] Drag-and-drop with snap functionality
- [ ] Visual magnitude supports (optional)
- [ ] Multiple game modes (place, order, compare)
- [ ] Strategy feedback based on number types
- [ ] Graduated difficulty levels
- [ ] Estimation mode for advanced learners
- [ ] Comparison strategy tracking

### Cross-Game Considerations

- [ ] Consistent visual language between games
- [ ] Shared student progress model
- [ ] Numbers transfer between games
- [ ] Parent/teacher reporting dashboard
- [ ] Session timing recommendations
- [ ] Accessibility features (color-blind modes, audio support)
